How to Get Funded by a Prop Firm: A Step-by-Step Guide

"Getting funded" has become the goal for a whole generation of retail traders — and a whole industry of evaluations has grown up around it. What "getting funded" actually means

A proprietary trading firm ("prop firm") gives skilled traders access to the firm's capital in exchange for a share of the profits. The modern retail version works like this: you pay a one-time fee to attempt an evaluation — a simulated account with a profit target and a strict set of risk rules. Clear the target without breaking a rule and the firm gives you a funded account, from which you keep the majority of any profit (commonly 80–90%).

Two honest points most marketing skips. First, the evaluation and, at most firms, the funded account itself are simulated — you're trading a demo balance, and the firm pays you a share of the profit your simulated trading would have made. You are never risking your own money beyond the evaluation fee. Second, the firm's business model only works if most people fail the evaluation and buy another. That's not a conspiracy — it just means the rules are designed to be genuinely hard, and your job is to treat the evaluation as a precision exercise in risk, not a get-rich-quick shortcut.

The mindset that gets funded

Passing an evaluation is a risk-management test with a profit target attached — not a profit contest. The traders who pass are rarely the most aggressive; they're the ones who never breach a limit.

Step 1 — Learn to actually trade first

This is the step everyone wants to skip and almost no one should. An evaluation fee is not a substitute for skill, and "funded" is not a strategy. Before you pay for a challenge you need a defined edge — a repeatable setup with rules for entry, exit and position size — and enough screen time to trade it without improvising.

If you're starting from zero, work through the fundamentals first: what actually moves a market, how to read price, how risk and position sizing work, and how a single number — risk per trade — governs everything downstream. Our free Trading 101 course covers the groundwork with no account required, and the wider Traxent learning path takes it through chart patterns, risk and prop-firm-specific rules.

A useful gate before spending on any evaluation: can you trade a demo account for 20+ sessions and stay within a self-imposed 4–5% max drawdown while making steady progress? If not, you're not being cheap by practising longer — you're avoiding paying repeated evaluation fees to learn a lesson a demo would have taught you for free.

Step 2 — Understand the evaluation model

Evaluations come in a few shapes, and the shape changes how you should trade it.

  • 1-step — a single phase: hit one profit target inside the rules and you're funded. Faster, but usually with tighter risk rules.
  • 2-step — two phases (often a larger target then a smaller one) before funding. Slower but typically more forgiving day to day. FTMO-style futures and forex programs are commonly 2-step; see the FTMO rules breakdown.
  • Instant / no-evaluation — pay more up front, skip the challenge, start on a funded (still usually simulated) account with strict rules. Less common and rule-heavy.

Whatever the shape, pin down four numbers before you start: the profit target, the maximum drawdown, the daily loss limit, and the minimum trading days. If you can't state all four from memory, you're not ready to trade that evaluation.

Step 3 — The rules that fail most traders

Most evaluations aren't failed on profitability — they're failed on a rule the trader didn't fully understand. Three deserve special attention.

Drawdown type is the big one

The type of maximum drawdown matters more than the number:

Drawdown typeHow it behavesWhat it punishes
StaticFixed from your starting balance; never moves.The most forgiving — you only fail if total loss hits the limit.
Trailing (intraday)Follows your equity up — including unrealised profit — then locks.Giving back open profit; a winner that reverses can breach you.
End-of-day (EOD) trailingTrails, but only updates at the session close.Overnight lock-in; feels roomy intraday, tightens each day.

A 10% static drawdown and a 10% trailing drawdown are completely different animals. Firms vary a lot here — compare how each one measures it on the side-by-side firm comparison before you choose.

The daily loss limit

A separate, hard line: lose more than X% in a single day and the evaluation ends, even if your overall drawdown is fine. This is why a hard daily stop — a loss figure at which you close the platform for the day — is non-negotiable.

The consistency rule

Many firms cap how much of your total profit can come from a single day (often ~30–50%). It exists to stop one lucky session from carrying the whole evaluation. Practically, it means you want several moderate green days, not one enormous one.

Why this matters

Two traders with identical strategies can get opposite results purely because one understood the drawdown type and consistency rule and the other didn't. The rules are the game.

Step 4 — Pick the firm that fits your style

The best firm isn't the one with the biggest account or the cheapest fee — it's the one whose rules match how you actually trade. A scalper who takes many small trades wants generous daily loss room and a static or EOD drawdown; a patient swing trader can live with tighter daily limits but cares about overnight and news rules.

Step 5 — Build an evaluation-proof plan

Turn the rules into hard numbers before you place a trade:

  1. Fixed risk per trade. Most funded traders risk 0.25–0.75% of the evaluation balance per trade. Smaller risk means more losing trades before you're near any limit.
  2. A hard daily stop below the firm's limit. If the daily loss limit is 5%, set your stop at 2–3% and honour it without exception.
  3. A drawdown buffer. Never trade into the last third of your max drawdown; treat it as the failure line, not a target.
  4. A consistency-aware target. Aim for steady daily gains that keep any single day under the consistency cap.
  5. A minimum-days plan. Don't rush the target and trip the minimum-days rule; pace the evaluation to the calendar.

Write these down as your own rulebook and treat breaking one of your numbers as the real failure — long before the firm's limit would.

Step 7 — Pass, get funded, protect the payout

Clearing the target is only half the job. On the funded phase, the same rules (or slightly different ones) still apply, and now there's a payout schedule and often a minimum days-to-payout requirement. The temptation after passing is to size up and "make it count" — which is exactly how newly funded traders breach a drawdown in week one.

Trade the funded account more conservatively than the evaluation, not less. Your goal shifts from hitting a target to stringing together consistent green days to your first payout, then repeating. Getting funded is the start line, not the finish.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to get funded?+

There's no fixed timeline. Firms set a minimum number of trading days (often 3–5) but most modern evaluations have no maximum, so it depends on your consistency. Building the skill to pass reliably usually takes months of practice; the evaluation itself can take a few weeks.

Is prop firm trading real money?+

The evaluation is a simulated (demo) account, and most funded accounts are simulated too. The firm pays you a share of the profit your simulated trading generates. You don't risk your own capital beyond the evaluation fee.

What's the hardest rule to pass?+

For most traders it's the drawdown rule combined with the consistency rule. Trailing and end-of-day drawdowns move as you profit and catch people out; consistency rules stop a single lucky day carrying the evaluation. Knowing exactly how yours is measured is the biggest single edge.

Can a complete beginner get funded?+

Yes, but rarely quickly. Expect months of learning and practice before an evaluation is a sensible spend. Getting funded is a skill test, not a lottery: learn, practise, prove readiness against the rules, then pay.
