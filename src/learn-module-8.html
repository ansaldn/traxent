Module 10 · Lesson 1

What a strategy actually is

Most people think a strategy is a prediction about where price will go. It isn't. A strategy is a set of written rules that tells you what to do — before, during and after every trade — so that no single decision depends on how you feel in the moment.

You already have most of the ingredients. The patterns from Chart Patterns are setups. The stop-loss and sizing maths from Risk Management is your risk rule. The journal loop from Review & Building Your Edge is your feedback system. A strategy is simply those pieces written down as one connected, testable procedure.

The five parts every strategy has

Strip away the jargon and every real trading strategy — from a beginner's first ruleset to a hedge fund's algorithm — answers the same five questions:

Anatomy of a strategy
1 · Market
What do I trade?
One instrument, one timeframe, defined sessions
2 · Setup
What must be true?
The conditions that qualify a trade at all
3 · Entry
What triggers it?
The exact, observable signal to act
4 · Exit
Where am I wrong / right?
Stop-loss AND profit target, decided in advance
5 · Risk
How much per trade?
Fixed risk, position sized from the stop distance

The test of a real strategy is brutal and simple: could a stranger trade it identically from your written rules alone? If any part needs your gut feel to interpret — "enter when it looks strong" — it isn't a rule yet, and it can't be tested, improved or trusted under pressure.

Key idea

A strategy doesn't predict the market. It standardises you. The market stays uncertain either way — the strategy removes your in-the-moment judgement as a variable, which is exactly what a prop firm evaluation is testing.

Module 10 · Lesson 2

The strategy families

Almost every strategy ever traded belongs to one of a few families, each built on a different belief about how price behaves. Knowing the families stops you reinventing wheels — and tells you which wheels suit a prop firm's rules.

The three core families

Strategy families and the belief behind each
Trend following
"What's moving keeps moving"
Buy strength, sell weakness · fewer, bigger wins · long losing streaks between them
Mean reversion
"What's stretched snaps back"
Fade extremes back to an average · many small wins · occasional sharp losses
Breakout / momentum
"Compression precedes expansion"
Enter as a range breaks · frequent false starts · pays on expansion days

Cutting across the families is holding time: scalping (seconds to minutes), intraday (closed by the session's end) and swing (days to weeks). The family says why you enter; the holding time says how long you're exposed.

Why this matters for funded accounts

Firm rules quietly favour some profiles. A trailing drawdown punishes trend strategies that give back open profit while riding a move. A consistency rule punishes styles that make a month in one day. A daily loss limit punishes mean-reversion's occasional sharp loss if your sizing is loose. None of this makes a family wrong — it means the pairing of strategy and firm is a decision, and it's exactly the pairing your readiness tracker measures from your real numbers.

Worked example

A swing trend-follower averaging one big winner a week would sail through FTMO's static drawdown but repeatedly trip Topstep's ~50% consistency check. The same trader flattening trades daily with fixed 1% risk fits both. Same skill — different strategy-to-rules fit.

The stack: firm, platform, data, you

Here's a fact that confuses almost every new funded trader: the prop firm you pay does not build the software you trade on. Understanding who provides what — the stack — explains a dozen things that otherwise seem mysterious.

A funded account is really four layers working together, usually owned by four different companies:

The funded trading stack
Layer 1 · The firm
Capital + rules
FTMO, Apex, Topstep… they fund you, set the limits, and pay out. That's ALL they provide.
Layer 2 · The platform
Execution software
Tradovate, NinjaTrader, cTrader, MT5, DXtrade — where your orders actually live
Layer 3 · Data & routing
Prices in, orders out
Exchange data feeds (e.g. CME) and the brokers/routers that carry orders
Layer 4 · You
The strategy
Your rules, executed on layer 2, judged against layer 1's limits

Even when it looks built-in, it usually isn't. Many firms embed a platform inside their own website with their logo on it — a white-label. Under the paint it's still cTrader, DXtrade or Match-Trader, run by the platform company. That's why two "different" firms can feel identical to trade on, and why your platform skills transfer between firms.

Why the layering matters to you

Three practical consequences. First, your strategy lives at the platform layer — learn Tradovate or TradingView once and you can carry the same strategy across many firms. Second, the firm only sees results — it judges your account against its rules; it doesn't care which buttons you pressed. Third, this is why Traxent connects to platforms, not firms: your fills live on the platform layer, so one read-only platform connection covers whichever firm's challenge you're running on it.

The factoid, in one line

Prop firms provide the capital and the rulebook. The platform provides the trading — even when it's wearing the firm's logo.

Manual vs automated — and the AI reality check

Every strategy sits somewhere on a spectrum: from a human reading rules off a checklist to code executing with no human at all. In the AI era you need to know what automation genuinely does well, what it doesn't — and what your firm allows.

The spectrum

From discretionary to fully automated
Discretionary
Human decides
Rules guide judgement; you click the buttons
Systematic manual
Rules decide, human executes
Zero interpretation; you're the executor
Semi-automated
Code signals, human confirms
Alerts fire; you approve each entry
Fully automated
Code decides and executes
A strategy script or bot trades unattended

What automation is genuinely good at: perfect discipline (code never revenge-trades), perfect consistency (the same rule executed the same way, every time), and testability (you can run a coded strategy against years of history in seconds). Notice what those are — the exact things Modules 8 and 9 taught you humans struggle with.

What it cannot do: know the future. A backtest shows how rules would have performed on data the market has already revealed — and an "AI strategy" is still a set of learned rules facing an uncertain tomorrow. The uncomfortable truth of the AI era: tools that make strategy-building easy make overfitting easy too — tuning rules so tightly to the past that they describe history instead of an edge. You'll learn to spot that in Lesson 6.

Check your firm's rules first

Automation policy is a firm rule like any other — and it varies widely. Some firms welcome EAs and bots, others ban fully-automated or copy trading outright, and many allow automation in evaluation but restrict it when funded. Before you ever deploy a strategy on a challenge, read your firm's automation policy the way you read its drawdown rule. Breaching it can void a passed evaluation.

Where we're heading

For the build in the next lesson we use semi- and fully-automated on paper only — the point is to watch your rules trade without you, which is the fastest way to see whether the rules alone hold up. What you deploy later, and where, is your call and your firm's rulebook.

Module 10 · Lesson 5

Build it: from idea to written rules

Time to build one. We'll take a deliberately simple, classic idea — a moving-average pullback in the direction of the trend — write it as five testable rules, and set it up on a free TradingView paper account so you can watch it trade in the next lesson.

Step 1 — write the rules

Our example strategy, written the way every strategy should be. Nothing here is a recommendation to trade real money — it's a teaching chassis, chosen because every part is simple enough to verify by eye:

The example ruleset — "MA pullback"

Market: one liquid instrument (our walkthrough uses an index future), 15-minute chart, main session only. Setup: price above the 50-period moving average — we only look for longs in an uptrend. Entry: price pulls back to touch the 20-period MA, then closes back above it. Exit: stop-loss below the pullback low; target at 2× the stop distance (1:2 R:R). Risk: 1% of the account per trade, position sized from the stop distance.

Read it against Lesson 1's five parts — market, setup, entry, exit, risk — and notice a stranger could trade it identically. That's the bar.

Step 2 — set up the platform

TradingView is our walkthrough platform because it's free, needs no broker, includes paper trading, and has a built-in strategy tester — a beginner can go from rules to a running paper strategy in one sitting:

1
Create a free TradingView account (tradingview.com) and open a chart for your instrument on the 15-minute timeframe.
2
Add the two moving averages — Indicators → search "Moving Average" → add one set to length 50 and one to length 20. You can now see your setup and entry conditions.
3
Open the Pine Editor (bottom panel) → New → Strategy. TradingView gives you a working strategy template immediately.
4
Describe the rules in Pine Script. The built-in template plus the editor's autocomplete gets you remarkably far: the conditions are close > ta.sma(close, 50) for the setup and a cross back above ta.sma(close, 20) for the entry, with strategy.exit setting the stop and the 1:2 target. Save, then Add to chart.
5
Look at the chart. Every historical entry and exit your rules would have taken is now painted on the price history — your first sight of the strategy trading without you.
If you'd rather not touch code

The strategy works identically as a manual checklist: keep the two MAs on the chart, and when the four conditions line up, place the trade yourself in TradingView's paper-trading panel (right-click the chart → Trade). Same rules, human executor — that's the "systematic manual" point on last lesson's spectrum, and it's how many funded traders operate permanently.

Test it: backtest, paper-trade, judge

A strategy you haven't tested is an opinion. Testing happens in two stages — against the past, then against the live-but-riskless present — and you already own the maths to judge the results.

Stage 1 — the backtest

With your strategy added to the chart, open the Strategy Tester panel. TradingView has already run your rules over the visible history. Four numbers matter, in this order:

Reading the Strategy Tester like a prop trader
1 · Max drawdown
Read this FIRST
Would this dip have breached your firm's daily loss or max drawdown? If yes, nothing else matters.
2 · Number of trades
Sample size
Under ~50 trades, the other numbers are mostly noise
3 · Win rate + avg win/loss
Your expectancy inputs
Feed them into Module 9's expectancy formula
4 · Profit factor
Gross wins ÷ gross losses
Above ~1.3 with a real sample is worth forward-testing
The overfitting trap

If you tweak settings until the backtest looks perfect — nudging the MA lengths, adding filters until every loser disappears — you haven't found an edge, you've memorised the past. A robust strategy performs acceptably across different settings and different periods; a curve-fit one performs brilliantly on exactly one configuration and collapses everywhere else. When in doubt, test the same rules on a time period you didn't build them on.

Stage 2 — the forward test

The backtest earns the strategy an audition, not a job. Now trade it on paper, in real time — TradingView's paper account executes your entries live with virtual money. Run it for at least a few weeks or ~20 trades, treating it exactly as seriously as Module 6's demo-trading lesson taught: realistic size, every rule honoured, every trade journalled.

This is where Traxent closes the loop: log the paper trades in your sim journal (or connect the platform when your integration goes live) and the readiness tracker scores your strategy's real behaviour against each firm's actual limits — drawdown shape, consistency, daily losses. The strategy stops being an idea and becomes a measured profile you can put in front of a specific firm's rulebook.

Other platforms, same recipe

Prefer to build where you'll eventually trade? The recipe is identical — rules, backtest, paper, judge — only the tooling changes. MetaTrader 5 automates via Expert Advisors with its own Strategy Tester; cTrader uses cBots (C#) with built-in backtesting; Tradovate has no native builder, so automation there runs through connected tools — most traders prototype on TradingView first, then execute on Tradovate manually or via a bridge. Whatever the venue: paper first, firm's automation policy checked, always.
